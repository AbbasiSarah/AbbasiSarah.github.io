Imagine your boss calls you on video. It's their face, their voice, their way of talking. They ask you to quietly send money for a secret deal. Would you say no? According to US prosecutors, many employees didn't, because the "boss" was made with AI.

## What happened

On **Thursday 5 November 2026**, prosecutors in the **Southern District of New York** announced charges against **three men** accused of stealing **about $129 million** from companies around the world. Their names are **Jacob Soall (41)**, **Sharon Hazan (54)** and **Guy Sheetrit (42)**, with ties to Spain, the UK, Israel and the UAE.

They face charges including **wire fraud** (stealing money using phone, email or online transfers), **aggravated identity theft** and **money laundering** (hiding where stolen money came from). All three were arrested in Spain or the UK between **June and September**, and the US has asked for them to be sent to New York. Remember: these are **allegations**, and they are presumed innocent until proven guilty.

## How the scam worked

Prosecutors say the scheme ran from about **July 2024 to February 2026** and followed the same steps:

1. An employee in the **accounting team** got an email that seemed to come from their **CEO**, asking for help with a confidential deal.
2. Next came a phone or video call where **AI copied the CEO's voice**, and sometimes their face. This is called a **deepfake**.
3. The employee was then sent to a "lawyer" at a big law firm. That lawyer was fake too. Soall allegedly played British lawyers using a handwritten script titled **"Lawyer Call."**
4. The fake lawyer told the employee where to wire the money. It was then turned into **cryptocurrency** to make it harder to trace.

Victims named in court papers include **hospital systems in Missouri and California**, a **Rhode Island bank**, a **UK fintech company** (which allegedly lost about **$11.58 million**), a mutual fund company and even a **pet food company**.

## Why it matters

This trick, pretending to be the boss, is very old. What's new is that AI made the fake **look and sound real**. An email can be ignored, but a familiar voice on a live call feels like proof. It isn't anymore.

One detail stood out: a large New York investment bank was also targeted, but its **back office stopped the payment** before it went out. Good checks worked.

## What we can learn

1. **A voice or face is not proof of identity.** If a call asks for money or secrets, hang up and call back on a number you already know.
2. **Secrecy is a red flag.** "Don't tell anyone" is how scammers stop you from checking.
3. **Processes protect people.** Two-person approval for big payments saves companies, not just smart employees.
4. **Builders have a role too.** If you work on voice or video AI, think about safety features like watermarks and consent checks from day one.
