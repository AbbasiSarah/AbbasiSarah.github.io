Every time we ask a chatbot a question, a computer somewhere does the work. Those computers live in **data centres**: huge buildings full of servers that need a lot of electricity, cooling and land. This weekend, people across Europe said: slow down.

## What happened

On **Friday 6 and Saturday 7 November 2026**, **more than 100 groups in 15 European countries** joined a campaign called **Stop Dirty Data Centres**. Events were planned in places including **Ireland, Poland, Denmark, Finland, Spain, the Netherlands, France and the UK**.

It wasn't only marches. Campaigners organised public meetings, petitions, walking tours, cycle rides and even poetry readings. In London, there was a photocall outside the energy ministry and a community day near a planned data centre on Brick Lane. A march to the Scottish Parliament is planned for **14 November**.

## What do they want?

Their main demand is an **immediate moratorium**, which means a temporary pause, on new **large-scale** data centre projects. They want clear rules first: should a site be built at all, and under what conditions? They also want local people to have a real say.

Their worries include:

- **higher energy bills** and pressure on the power grid;
- **water use**, since many data centres use water for cooling;
- **noise**, construction traffic and pollution from backup generators;
- the loss of **land**, including farmland.

## Why it matters

The numbers are growing fast:

- In **Ireland**, data centres used **23% of all metered electricity in 2025**, and the grid operator EirGrid says this could reach **30% by 2030**.
- Yearly data centre investment in Europe has roughly doubled, from **€12 billion in 2020 to about €25–26 billion in 2026**, according to a PwC report cited by Euronews.
- In Devon, England, one planned campus would need **1.5 gigawatts** of power (about the output of a large power station) on **850 acres** of farmland.

Governments are in a tricky spot. Many, like the UK, see AI as a path to economic growth and want more data centres, not fewer. But voters are paying attention. Data centres were also a big issue in the recent **US midterm elections**, where one analyst called them an "opportunistic political football."

## What we can learn

1. **AI is not "in the cloud." It's in a building.** Every model runs on real hardware that uses real energy and water.
2. **Efficiency is a skill.** Smaller models, better code and fewer wasted runs all reduce the load. As learners, we can practise this from day one.
3. **Tech and policy go together.** The future of AI won't be decided only by engineers. Communities, lawmakers and voters are joining the conversation, and people who understand both sides will be needed.
