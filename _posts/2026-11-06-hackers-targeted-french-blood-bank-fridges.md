Two weeks after [hackers overheated fridges in 700 French supermarkets](/blog/hackers-heated-french-supermarket-fridges/), the same kind of attack hit something far more serious: **blood**.

## What happened

On the night of **1–2 November 2026**, attackers got into the systems that monitor and control the **cold storage rooms** at three sites of the **French Blood Establishment (EFS)**, France's national blood service. They pushed the temperatures up to levels that would ruin blood and blood products.

It didn't work:

- **Alarms went off** as soon as the temperatures started rising.
- Staff, who work around the clock because blood can be needed at any time, **moved the blood to other fridges** that weren't connected to the hacked system.
- EFS says **no blood products were lost**, no patient was harmed, and blood collection and deliveries carried on as normal.

## How did they get in?

EFS said the attackers came in through a **service provider**, the company that supplies and remotely monitors the cold rooms. Media reports name the supplier as **Coolworld**, which blamed a component from another supplier in its remote-monitoring software.

A group described as Russia-aligned claimed it was behind the attacks and said it only wanted to expose bad security. Investigators have not confirmed who did it.

## It was predicted

In October, the head of France's cybersecurity agency (ANSSI), **Vincent Strubel**, publicly warned that refrigeration systems storing **medical supplies** could be the next target. About two weeks later, they were.

## What we can learn

1. **Alarms and people still matter.** The technology failed, but an independent alarm and a trained night shift stopped a disaster. Good security plans assume something will break.
2. **Your security is only as strong as your suppliers'.** EFS wasn't hacked directly; the company it trusted to monitor its fridges was.
3. **Critical equipment shouldn't sit openly on the internet.** Remote monitoring is useful, but it should run through a secure, private connection.

When attacks move from stealing data to changing the physical world, such as food, medicine or blood, cybersecurity becomes a public-safety issue.
